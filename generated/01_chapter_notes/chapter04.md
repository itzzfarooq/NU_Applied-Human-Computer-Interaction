# Chapter 4: Paradigms — The Big Ideas That Shaped HCI

## The Big Picture

This chapter is a **history of ideas** in HCI. It traces the paradigm shifts — the fundamental worldview changes — that transformed computing from a machine for specialists to something embedded in everyday life. Each paradigm changed **what we thought computers were for** and **how we expected to interact with them**.

The arc: from **impersonal batch processing → personal, interactive, ubiquitous computing**.

---

## 1. THE PARADIGM SHIFTS — A Timeline

| Era | Paradigm | Key Idea | What Changed |
|-----|----------|----------|-------------|
| 1940s-50s | **Batch Processing** | Submit job, wait for results | Impersonal, no direct interaction |
| 1960s | **Time-Sharing** | One computer, many users | **Interactive computing** is born |
| 1960s-70s | **Networking** | Many computers connected | Community computing, email, file sharing |
| 1962 | **Graphical Displays** | Sutherland's Sketchpad | Visualizing & manipulating data; **direct manipulation** |
| 1970s | **Microprocessor** | Personal, dedicated machines | Kay's Dynabook vision; **personal computing** |
| 1990s | **WWW** | Hypertext on global network | **Global information**; critical mass transformed economy |
| 1991+ | **Ubiquitous Computing** | Technology that disappears | **Symbiosis** of physical and electronic worlds |

---

## 2. THE INITIAL PARADIGM: BATCH PROCESSING

Computing started as **impersonal** — you submitted a job (on punch cards or tape), waited hours or days, and got results. No interaction during execution. The computer was not a partner in dialogue; it was a tool you used indirectly.

---

## 3. TIME-SHARING — The First Paradigm Shift

J.C.R. Licklider at ARPA championed the idea: a single computer supporting **multiple users simultaneously** through interactive terminals. This was the birth of **interactive computing** — the user could now talk to the computer in real time.

---

## 4. NETWORKING — From Individual to Community

Multiple computers connected. Suddenly you could **email, share files, collaborate** across machines. Computing became social — a community activity, not just an individual one.

---

## 5. GRAPHICAL DISPLAYS — Seeing Is Interacting

In 1962, Ivan Sutherland's **Sketchpad** demonstrated that computers could be used for **visualizing and manipulating data** — not just crunching numbers. The user could draw on a screen and the computer would respond. This was the seed of **direct manipulation**: interacting with a visual representation of your work rather than typing commands.

**Key insight**: One person's contribution (Sutherland) could drastically change the history of computing.

---

## 6. THE MICROPROCESSOR — Computing for Everyone

In the 1970s, Seymour Papert's **LOGO** language let children write simple graphics programs. The philosophy: **a system is more powerful as it becomes easier to use.**

Alan Kay at Xerox PARC envisioned the **Dynabook** — the ultimate personal computer, a portable device for creative thought. This vision drove the development of personal computing: small, powerful machines dedicated to the individual.

---

## 7. THE WORLD WIDE WEB — Global Information at Your Fingertips

Tim Berners-Lee combined hypertext with the global network. The key was **simplicity**: HTTP and HTML were universal, easy to implement protocols. When a **critical mass** of users adopted it, the information economy was completely transformed.

---

## 8. UBIQUITOUS COMPUTING — The Technology That Disappears

Mark Weiser (1991): "The most profound technologies are those that disappear."

In the late 1980s, the computer was very **apparent** — it sat on your desk and demanded your attention. Weiser asked: **how to make it disappear?** Answer: shrink it and embed it in the physical world. Design interactions that don't demand our intention.

**Goal**: Symbiosis of physical and electronic worlds in service of everyday activities.

---

## 9. ALONGSIDE THE SHIFTS — Key Developments

### Window Systems & WIMP

The Xerox Star (1981) was the first commercial windowing system. Humans can pursue multiple tasks at once — windows let you "change the topic" by switching between independent screen areas. Windows, icons, menus, and pointers became the familiar interaction mechanisms we still use today.

### Metaphor — Relating Computers to Real Life

Metaphors are effective teaching techniques:
- LOGO's "turtle" dragging its tail
- Desktop metaphor for file management
- Word processing as typing
- Spreadsheets for financial analysis
- Virtual reality — user *inside* the metaphor

**Problems**: Some tasks don't fit a given metaphor. Metaphors carry cultural bias.

### Direct Manipulation (Shneiderman, 1982)

The appeal of graphically-based interaction comes from:
1. **Visibility** of objects
2. **Incremental action** and rapid feedback
3. **Reversibility** — encourages exploration
4. **Syntactic correctness** — all actions are valid
5. **Replace language with action**

The Apple Macintosh (1984) brought this to the mainstream. **WYSIWYG** (What You See Is What You Get) — the screen shows exactly what you'll get.

### Language vs. Action — A Tension

| Paradigm | How It Works | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Direct Manipulation** | Interface as a "world" you act upon | Drag file to trash |
| **Language-based** | Interface as mediator/agent | Type "delete file.txt" |
| **Programming by example** | Both action and language | Record macro, then replay |

### Hypertext — Non-Linear Information

**1945**: Vannevar Bush's **memex** — a vision for managing information explosion.
**1960s**: Ted Nelson describes hypertext as a **non-linear browsing structure**.
Hypermedia and multimedia extend this to images, audio, video.
Nelson's Xanadu (a universal hypertext system) remains a dream.

### Multimodality — Using Multiple Channels

A **mode** is a human communication channel. Multimodality = simultaneous use of multiple channels for input and output (e.g., speaking + pointing + seeing).

### CSCW — Computer Supported Cooperative Work

CSCW removes the bias of **single user / single computer** systems. It recognizes that work is social. Electronic mail is the most prominent success.

### Agent-Based Interfaces — A Return to Language

The evolution:
1. **Original**: commands given to computer (language-based)
2. **Direct Manipulation / WIMP**: actions performed on a "world" representation (action-based)
3. **Agents**: return to language by instilling **proactivity and intelligence** in the command processor

Agents appear as avatars, natural language processors, and intelligent assistants.

### Sensor-Based & Context-Aware Interaction

Humans are good at recognizing context and reacting appropriately. As sensors (light, temperature, location, identity) become cheaper and easier, the question becomes: **how do we go from sensed physical measures to interactions that behave as if "aware" of the surroundings?**

5. **LOGO** (1970s, Papert): programming for children
6. **Dynabook** (Kay, Xerox PARC): the ultimate personal computer vision
7. **Xerox Star** (1981): first commercial windowing system
8. **Metaphor**: relating computing to real life — problems: cultural bias, task mismatch
9. **Direct manipulation** (Shneiderman, 1982): visibility, incremental action, rapid feedback, reversibility
10. **WYSIWYG**: What You See Is What You Get
11. **Hypertext**: Bush's memex (1945) → Nelson's Xanadu — non-linear browsing
12. **Multimodality**: simultaneous use of multiple communication channels
13. **CSCW**: removes single-user bias, work is social
14. **Agent-based interfaces**: return to language with proactivity and intelligence
15. **Ubiquitous computing** (Weiser, 1991): technology that disappears, embedded in physical world
