### Chapter 1: The Human

**36.** Explain the difference between episodic and semantic long-term memory, and describe how semantic memory is derived from episodic memory.

> **Answer:** Episodic memory stores personal experiences and events in a serial, temporal fashion — it is "what happened when." Semantic memory stores structured knowledge: facts, concepts, and skills, organized in networks or frames. Semantic memory is derived from episodic memory through abstraction — over time, repeated episodic experiences are generalized into structured knowledge. For example, many individual visits to restaurants (episodic) eventually form a general "restaurant script" (semantic) containing expected roles, scenes, and props.

---

**37.** Describe the three types of reasoning (deduction, induction, abduction) and explain why each can be unreliable.

> **Answer:** Deduction derives logically necessary conclusions from premises. It is unreliable because logical validity does not guarantee factual truth — the premises themselves may be false. Induction generalizes from observed cases to unobserved cases. It is unreliable because it can only prove false, never true (the "problem of induction"), and humans are poor at using negative evidence. Abduction reasons from an event to its most likely cause. It is unreliable because multiple causes could explain the same event, leading to false explanations. Example: seeing someone run fast and inferring they are exercising, when they might actually be fleeing danger.

---

**38.** Explain what a "script" model of semantic memory is and give an example of its components.

> **Answer:** A script is a model of stereotypical information required to interpret a situation. It contains: entry conditions (what must be true before the script runs), result (expected outcome), props (objects involved), roles (actors and their actions), scenes (sequential steps), and tracks (variations for different circumstances). Example: a "visit to the doctor" script has entry conditions (patient is ill, clinic is open), roles (doctor examines, patient describes symptoms), scenes (reception, waiting room, examination), and props (examination table, medical instruments). Scripts help us understand and predict situations efficiently.

---

### Chapter 3: The Interaction

**39.** Using Norman's seven-stage model, trace what happens when a user tries to save a document but clicks "Cancel" instead of "Save." Identify which stage the error occurs at and whether it is a slip or a mistake.

> **Answer:** The user's goal is to save the document. They formulate the intention to save, but at the stage of specifying actions at the interface, they specify the wrong action (clicking "Cancel" instead of "Save"). The action is then executed. This is a **slip** — the user had the correct intention (save) and understood the system, but the wrong action was performed. The fix is better interface design, such as placing "Save" in a more prominent position or adding confirmation for destructive actions like "Cancel."

---

**40.** Compare direct manipulation and indirect manipulation, giving an example of each and explaining when each is appropriate.

> **Answer:** Direct manipulation involves the user interacting with an artificial world on screen — they directly manipulate objects (e.g., dragging a file icon to a trash can). It is appropriate for office/desktop applications where the user works within the computer's world. Indirect manipulation involves the user interacting with the *real* world *through* the interface — for example, adjusting a temperature setting on a industrial control panel that controls actual heating equipment. It is appropriate in industrial settings where the interface mediates between the user and physical processes. The key difference is whether the user manipulates virtual objects (direct) or real-world objects via an interface layer (indirect).

### Chapter 5: Interaction Design Basics

**41.** Explain the four navigation golden rules (landmarks, routes, overviews, context) and give a practical example of each in a web application.

> **Answer:** (1) **Landmarks** help users know where they are — e.g., a highlighted current page in the navigation bar. (2) **Routes** provide clear paths through information — e.g., a breadcrumb trail showing "Home > Products > Laptops." (3) **Overviews** show the overall structure — e.g., a sitemap or a collapsible sidebar menu showing all sections. (4) **Context** shows relationship to other parts — e.g., "Related Products" links or a "Back to Category" button. Together, these rules help users orient themselves, navigate efficiently, and understand where they are in the information space.

---

**42.** What is the aesthetic-usability effect, and why is it relevant to HCI design?

> **Answer:** The aesthetic-usability effect is the phenomenon where users perceive aesthetically pleasing designs as more usable than less attractive ones, even when objective usability is the same. This is relevant to HCI because aesthetics influence user trust, satisfaction, and willingness to forgive design flaws. Positive affect from attractive interfaces promotes creative problem-solving and makes users more tolerant of minor issues. However, designers must not rely solely on aesthetics — genuine usability (learnability, efficiency, error prevention) remains essential. The effect underscores that emotional response and visual design are not superficial but integral to user experience.

### Chapter 6: HCI in the Software Process

**43.** Explain the difference between throwaway, incremental, and evolutionary prototypes. When would you choose each?

> **Answer:** **Throwaway** prototypes are built quickly and discarded after use — choose when exploring early design ideas or validating requirements before committing to implementation. **Incremental** prototypes are built piece by piece and added to the growing system — choose when the system can be developed in modules and each module can be tested independently. **Evolutionary** prototypes evolve gradually into the final system — choose when requirements are uncertain and you want to refine the design through continuous user feedback without starting from scratch. The choice depends on requirement clarity, time constraints, and whether the prototype architecture can support eventual production quality.

---

**44.** Describe the IBIS (Issue-Based Information System) method for design rationale. What are its three main elements?

> **Answer:** IBIS is a process-oriented design rationale method. Its three main elements are: (1) **Issues** — the key questions or problems being addressed, organized hierarchically with one root issue; (2) **Positions** — potential resolutions or responses to an issue; and (3) **Arguments** — which support or object to specific positions, modifying the relationship between positions and issues. In the graphical version (gIBIS), these are represented as nodes: an issue node is central, position nodes respond to it, argument nodes attach to positions, and sub-issues can specialize, generalize, or question the main issue. IBIS helps teams document and communicate the reasoning behind design decisions.

### Chapter 7: Design Rules

**45.** Compare and contrast Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules and Norman's 7 Principles. How do they complement each other?

> **Answer:** Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules are practical, action-oriented guidelines for interface design: consistency, shortcuts, feedback, closure, error prevention, reversal, locus of control, and reduced memory load. They are broad-brush heuristics useful as checklists. Norman's 7 Principles are more conceptual and rooted in cognitive science: use knowledge in world/head, simplify tasks, make things visible (bridge gulfs), get mappings right, exploit constraints, design for error, and standardize. They complement each other because Norman's principles explain *why* good design works (cognitive foundations), while Shneiderman's rules describe *what* to do (practical application). Together they provide both theoretical grounding and actionable guidance.

---

**46.** Explain the trade-off between principles, standards, and guidelines as types of design rules.

> **Answer:** These three types differ along two axes: generality and authority. **Principles** are abstract, highly general, but have low authority — they apply broadly but are open to interpretation. **Standards** are specific, have high authority (set by national/international bodies like ISO), but limited application — they mandate particular solutions. **Guidelines** fall in between: more specific than principles but less authoritative than standards, offering suggestive advice. The trade-off is that increasing generality decreases authority and vice versa. Effective design uses all three: principles for early conceptual work, guidelines for detailed design, and standards for compliance in specific contexts.

### Chapter 8: Implementation Support

**47.** Explain the difference between the read-evaluation loop and notification-based programming paradigms. Give an example of when each is more appropriate.

> **Answer:** In the **read-evaluation loop**, the program repeatedly reads an event, determines its type via a case/switch statement, and processes it — the application controls the flow. It is simple and predictable. In **notification-based** programming, the application registers callback functions with a notifier, and the notifier dispatches events to the appropriate handler — the system controls the flow. The read-evaluation loop is better for **modal** dialogs (easy to add an inner loop) but worse for **non-modal** dialogs (complex main loop). Notification-based is better for **non-modal** dialogs (just add a handler) but worse for **modal** dialogs (need mode flags). The choice should be driven by design needs, not implementation convenience.

---

**48.** Describe the Seeheim model and explain the role of its three layers.

> **Answer:** The Seeheim model is a conceptual architecture for UIMS with three layers: (1) **Presentation** (lexical level) — handles input/output at the device level, managing how information is displayed and how raw input is received. (2) **Dialogue Control** (syntactic level) — mediates between presentation and functionality, managing the sequence and structure of interaction (e.g., menu selection sequences). (3) **Functionality** (semantic level) — the application interface that performs the actual tasks. A **switch** component connects presentation and functionality. The model's contribution is conceptual: it separates concerns so that changes in presentation don't affect functionality and vice versa, improving portability, reusability, and customizability.

---

**49.** What is the MVC (Model-View-Controller) architecture, and what is its main practical limitation?

> **Answer:** MVC separates a component into three parts: **Model** (internal state and logic), **View** (how it is rendered on screen), and **Controller** (processes user input). Data flows from Model to View for display, from Controller to Model for updates, and View and Controller coordinate. The main practical limitation is that in graphical interfaces, input only has meaning in relation to output — a mouse click's meaning depends on what is displayed where. This means the Controller needs to know about the View, so in practice the separation is not complete and the Controller "talks" to the View. Despite this, MVC is widely used (e.g., Java Swing) because it still provides useful separation of concerns.

### Chapter 9: Evaluation Techniques

**50.** Compare within-groups and between-groups experimental designs. What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?

> **Answer:** In a **within-groups design**, each subject performs under every experimental condition. Advantages: fewer users needed, less costly, less affected by user variation. Disadvantages: transfer/learning effects between conditions can bias results (e.g., learning task A helps with task B). In a **between-groups design**, each subject performs under only one condition. Advantages: no transfer of learning, cleaner results. Disadvantages: requires more users, more expensive, and individual variation between groups can introduce bias. The choice depends on whether transfer effects are a serious concern (use between-groups), budget constraints (use within-groups), and whether the task is sensitive to learning effects.

---

**51.** Explain the difference between parametric and non-parametric statistical tests. When would you choose each?

> **Answer:** **Parametric tests** (e.g., t-test, ANOVA) assume the data follows a normal distribution. They are more powerful (better at detecting real effects) and robust when assumptions are met. **Non-parametric tests** (e.g., Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon) do not assume a normal distribution. They are less powerful but more reliable when data is skewed, ordinal, or violates parametric assumptions. Choose parametric when you have continuous data that is approximately normally distributed with reasonable sample size. Choose non-parametric when data is ordinal (e.g., Likert scales), has outliers, is heavily skewed, or the sample is small. In HCI, many measures (error counts, satisfaction ratings) are non-normal, making non-parametric tests common.

---

**52.** Describe the think aloud method and cooperative evaluation. How do they differ?

> **Answer:** **Think aloud** involves observing a user performing a task while the user verbally describes what they are doing, thinking, and observing. It is simple to conduct and provides insight into user reasoning, but is subjective, selective, and the act of describing may alter task performance. **Cooperative evaluation** is a variation where the user and evaluator collaborate throughout the session — both can ask questions, the user is encouraged to criticize the system, and clarification is possible. It is less constrained, easier for the user, and produces richer data. The key difference is that think aloud is a one-directional protocol (user reports to evaluator), while cooperative evaluation is a dialogue where both parties actively participate.

### Chapter 1: The Human

**53.** "Human memory is not a single system but comprises multiple interacting components." Explain this statement by describing the three types of memory (sensory, short-term, long-term), their key characteristics, and how they interact. Discuss the implications for interface design.

> **Answer:** Human memory consists of three interacting systems. **Sensory memory** (iconic for vision, echoic for hearing, haptic for touch) briefly buffers incoming stimuli but is continuously overwritten — it acts as a first filter. Through *attention*, selected stimuli enter **short-term memory (STM)**, which has rapid access (~70ms), rapid decay (~200ms), and limited capacity of 7±2 chunks. STM is a scratchpad for active processing. Through *rehearsal*, information moves to **long-term memory (LTM)**, which has slow access (~100ms) but huge/unlimited capacity and very slow decay. LTM stores both episodic (events) and semantic (facts, concepts) knowledge.
>
> The interaction is crucial: sensory memory filters, STM processes, and LTM stores. Design implications include: (1) chunk information to fit STM limits (e.g., phone numbers grouped as 0121 414 2626); (2) use meaningful categories to aid LTM encoding; (3) provide visible system state to reduce STM load (users don't need to remember what the system is doing); (4) use recognition rather than recall (menus vs. command lines) because recognition is less demanding on STM; (5) design for the distribution of practice effect by allowing users to spread learning over time.

### Chapter 3: The Interaction

**54.** Compare Norman's model and the Abowd and Beale framework as tools for understanding interaction. What does each model emphasize, and what are their limitations?

> **Answer:** **Norman's model** describes interaction as a seven-stage cycle: goal → intention → specify actions → execute → perceive → interpret → evaluate. It emphasizes the *user's cognitive perspective* — how the user formulates and evaluates actions. Its key contributions are the Execution/Evaluation loop and the concepts of the Gulf of Execution (user's actions ≠ system's allowed actions) and Gulf of Evaluation (expected state ≠ actual state). Limitations: it is user-centric and does not explicitly model the system's internal processes; it treats interaction as linear stages, which oversimplifies concurrent or exploratory interaction.
>
> The **Abowd and Beale framework** models interaction as translation between four languages: User (U), Input (I), System (S), and Output (O). It emphasizes that *each component has its own language* and problems arise from translation failures. It is more general — applicable beyond electronic computers to any interactive system — and identifies all major components. Limitations: it is abstract and does not provide detailed cognitive steps; it may be less useful for analyzing specific usability problems at the detail level.
>
> Together, they complement each other: Norman's model is better for understanding *why* a user struggles (cognitive analysis), while Abowd & Beale is better for understanding *where* in the system the breakdown occurs (architectural analysis).

### Chapter 5: Interaction Design Basics

**55.** Explain how Gestalt principles (proximity, similarity, closure, continuity) apply to screen design. Provide examples of each principle in a real or hypothetical interface, and discuss what happens when these principles are violated.

> **Answer:** Gestalt principles describe how humans perceptually organize visual information. **Proximity**: items close together are grouped — e.g., in a form, labels placed near their input fields are perceived as belonging together; violating this (placing labels far from fields) causes confusion about which label matches which field. **Similarity**: items that look alike are grouped — e.g., buttons with the same color and shape are perceived as belonging to the same function group; violating this (making unrelated buttons look identical) misleads users about functionality. **Closure**: the mind completes incomplete shapes — e.g., a progress bar that is partially filled is perceived as a complete bar with a gap; violating this (using ambiguous incomplete shapes) can cause misinterpretation. **Continuity**: the eye follows smooth paths — e.g., a timeline or flowchart with connected elements guides the eye naturally; violating this (abrupt breaks in flow) disrupts the user's visual tracking. These principles are fundamental to creating interfaces that are visually organized and cognitively manageable.

### Chapter 6: HCI in the Software Process

**56.** Why is the traditional waterfall model inadequate for interactive system development? Describe an alternative approach and explain how it addresses the limitations of the waterfall model.

> **Answer:** The waterfall model assumes a linear, sequential progression through stages: requirements → architectural design → detailed design → coding → integration → maintenance. This is inadequate for interactive systems because: (1) requirements are often incomplete or unknown at the start — users cannot articulate needs without seeing a system; (2) design decisions affect later stages in non-linear ways — a UI choice may invalidate earlier requirements; (3) user feedback is essential but the waterfall model delays testing until late in the process; (4) there are multiple feedback loops between all stages that the model does not accommodate.
>
> An **iterative design and prototyping** approach addresses these limitations. Instead of completing each stage fully before moving on, designers create prototypes (throwaway, incremental, or evolutionary) and refine them through cycles of testing, learning, and improving. This allows early user feedback, accommodates changing requirements, and catches usability problems before full implementation. The process is cyclical rather than linear, with continuous evaluation driving design refinement. This approach also supports usability engineering by making specific usability measures explicit requirements that can be tested at each iteration.

### Chapter 7: Design Rules

**57.** "Design rules exist on a spectrum from abstract principles to concrete standards." Discuss this statement by explaining the three types of design rules (principles, standards, guidelines), their characteristics, and when each is most useful in the design process.

> **Answer:** Design rules form a spectrum defined by two key dimensions: **generality** (how broadly applicable) and **authority** (how mandatory). At one end are **principles** — abstract, highly general, but low authority. Examples include Norman's "Make things visible" or "Design for error." Principles are useful in early design stages when making high-level architectural decisions; they guide thinking but do not prescribe specific solutions. In the middle are **guidelines** — more specific than principles, lower authority than standards, but broader application. Examples include "Use meaningful menu labels" or "Group related controls together." Guidelines are useful during detailed design when concrete decisions need to be made but flexibility is still needed. At the other end are **standards** — specific, high authority (set by bodies like ISO), but limited application. Examples include ISO 9241 (defining usability as effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction) or specific font size requirements. Standards are useful for compliance, consistency across products, and legal/regulatory requirements.
>
> The trade-off is that as rules become more specific (high authority), they become less applicable to diverse contexts. Effective design uses all three: principles set the vision, guidelines inform decisions, and standards ensure compliance. Understanding the justification for each type helps designers resolve conflicts when guidelines or standards contradict each other.

### Chapter 8: Implementation Support

**58.** Compare the MVC and PAC architectural models for interactive systems. What are the key components of each, and in what situations would you prefer one over the other?

> **Answer:** **MVC (Model-View-Controller)** separates a component into: Model (internal state and logic), View (screen rendering), and Controller (input processing). Data flows from Model to View (display), Controller to Model (update), and View coordinates with Controller. MVC originated in Smalltalk and is widely used (e.g., Java Swing). Its practical limitation is that in graphical interfaces, input depends on what is displayed, so the Controller must communicate with the View, breaking the clean separation.
>
> **PAC (Presentation-Abstraction-Control)** has three corresponding components: Abstraction (logical state, like Model), Presentation (manages I/O, combining View and Controller), and Control (mediates between Abstraction and Presentation). PAC is closer to the Seeheim model and manages hierarchy and multiple views more cleanly. The Control components of different PAC objects communicate with each other.
>
> **Preference**: Use MVC when working with object-oriented toolkits (Java Swing, web frameworks) where the ecosystem supports it and the component-level separation is sufficient. Use PAC when you need cleaner separation between I/O and logic, when managing complex hierarchies of components, or when multiple views of the same data need coordinated control. In practice, MVC is more common due to ecosystem support, but PAC is architecturally cleaner in many scenarios.

### Chapter 9: Evaluation Techniques

**59.** You are evaluating a new mobile banking app. Design an evaluation plan that combines at least three different evaluation methods. Justify your choice of methods, explain what each method will reveal, and discuss how the results will complement each other.

> **Answer:** A comprehensive evaluation plan should combine expert-based and user-based methods:
>
> **1. Heuristic Evaluation (expert-based):** 3–5 usability experts examine the app against Nielsen's 10 heuristics (consistency, feedback, error prevention, etc.). This will identify concrete usability violations early, before user testing. It is cost-effective and provides a systematic checklist. It reveals design-level problems but may miss context-specific issues.
>
> **2. Think Aloud User Testing (user-based, laboratory):** 8–10 representative users perform key tasks (transfer money, check balance, pay bills) while thinking aloud. This reveals *actual* user behavior, mental models, and pain points that experts might miss. It provides rich qualitative data about *why* problems occur. Laboratory setting allows controlled conditions and screen recording.
>
> **3. Questionnaire/Survey (query technique):** After task completion, users complete a System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire and open-ended satisfaction questions. This provides quantitative usability scores and subjective satisfaction data, allowing comparison with benchmarks and tracking improvement across iterations.
>
> **Complementarity:** Heuristic evaluation catches structural design issues cheaply. Think aloud reveals behavioral and cognitive issues in context. Questionnaires provide quantifiable metrics for reporting and comparison. Together they cover design, behavior, and perception — triangulating findings to produce a robust evaluation. The combination also balances qualitative depth with quantitative breadth.

**60.** "Evaluation should happen throughout the design process, not just at the end." Critically discuss this statement, explaining why early evaluation is valuable and describing how different evaluation methods are appropriate at different stages.

> **Answer:** This statement reflects a core principle of user-centered design. Early evaluation is valuable because: (1) **cost of change increases exponentially** over the lifecycle — fixing a problem in a paper prototype costs almost nothing, but fixing it after deployment can require rewriting code, retraining users, and losing trust; (2) **requirements are uncertain** — early evaluation validates whether the design direction is correct before committing resources; (3) **design inertia** — early bad decisions tend to persist, so catching them early prevents compounding errors.
>
> Different methods suit different stages:
> - **Early design (no implementation):** Cognitive walkthrough and heuristic evaluation can assess designs without any code. Paper prototyping and storyboards allow quick exploration of alternatives.
> - **Mid-development (prototypes):** Think aloud testing with low-fidelity prototypes reveals usability problems cheaply. Wizard of Oz techniques simulate functionality that doesn't yet exist.
> - **Post-implementation:** Field studies observe the system in its real context. Physiological measures (eye tracking, GSR) provide objective data. Questionnaires and interviews capture subjective satisfaction.
> - **Post-deployment:** Longitudinal field studies track long-term use, learning effects, and organizational impact.
>
> The key insight is that evaluation is not a single event but a continuous process that adapts its methods to the maturity of the design. Each stage has different evaluation goals (explore → validate → measure → monitor) and appropriate methods.
